Holographic acoustic levitation using phased arrays of transducers has facilitated innovative mid-air particle displays. However, current time-invariant methods for computing acoustic holograms often induce high phase changes and thus transducer amplitude fluctuations, severely limiting possible dynamic displays. In this work, we develop a wide range of temporal phase retrieval algorithms that suppress phase change and trade off computational time and solution optimality. We base our hardware implementation on Gerchberg-Saxton (GS), which we identify as gradient descent for maximizing focal amplitudes and thus acoustic trap quality. Following this, we adapt GS to additionally constrain transducers’ (and points’) phase changes from the previous time frame, and enable multiparticle animations without prior knowledge of particles’ paths and interactions. We experimentally showcase a series of levitated character animations depicting human motion and believe our work paves the way for natural dynamic multi-particle displays and unencumbered 3D delivery of other modalities such as haptics and audio.

1 INTRODUCTION

In recent years, acoustic levitation using phased arrays of transducers (PATs) has facilitated new types of particle-based displays, where trapped particles act as display voxels [Ochiai et al. 2014], or anchors for manipulation of other artifacts in mid-air [Morales et al. 2019]. These displays, initially limited to specific particle locations and arrangements [Ochiai et al. 2014], have evolved with holographic wavefront control, allowing arbitrary placement and movement of displayed particles in 3D space [Marzo and Drinkwater 2019; Marzo et al. 2015].

In these early works, acoustic holography optimized amplitudes at focal points (or traps), while satisfying constraints on the transducers’ amplitudes, to compute (usually) phase-only holograms that generate high focal amplitudes without knowledge of the focal phases. Repeating such computations over space and time has allowed impressive novel displays. Fast-moving particles have been shown to achieve the persistence of vision effect and generate volumetric visual content [Fushimi et al. 2019], which has been combined with haptics [Long et al. 2014] and audible sound [Ochiai et al. 2017] in multimodal displays [Hirayama et al. 2019; Plasencia et al. 2020]. Such displays are currently possible in more realistic environments that include rigid obstacles [Hirayama et al. 2022].

Despite this progress, the time-invariant computation of successive acoustic holograms hinders the potential for unrestricted 3D content delivery. While it is possible to place or move particles to complex formations such as an icosahedron [Marzo and Drinkwater 2019] or a human figure (Figure 1), levitated particles that share or are close to sharing their standing wave axes, can currently move as a group (see Supporting Movie 1 from [Marzo and Drinkwater 2019]), but lack full capability to move independently within the 3D space as demonstrated in Figure 1. For such demanding trajectories, where particles move in opposing directions along the transducer propagation direction, focal phases can vary considerably between successive time frames when using time-invariant phase retrieval (PR). Realizing such fields using a PAT transfers these phase changes to the transducers, which causes fluctuation in their emitted amplitude [Suzuki et al. 2020], eventually leading to weaker acoustic traps and particle drops.

Here we propose temporal PR algorithms that both succeed target amplitudes and suppress phase change. To comply with standard speed requirements of multimodal displays [Montano-Murillo et al. 2023], we modify the seminal Gerchberg-Saxton (GS) algorithm [Gerchberg 1972] to additionally suppress the transducers’ and focal points’ phase changes. We show that GS is the fastest method for generating quality acoustic traps as a gradient maximizer of focal amplitudes with hologram energy (Tikhonov) regularization. On the other side of the trade-off between computation time and solution optimality, we also introduce saddle point optimization in computer generated holography (CGH) to optimize the conflicting amplitude and phase objectives.

Our algorithms enable 3D particle displays that are not geometrically restricted in any way beyond the necessary separation between individual traps. We experimentally demonstrate particle displays that feature character animations, reminiscent of the well-known point-light displays used to explore the visual perception of biological motion [Johansson 1973]. We display mid-air human characters with 10 polystyrene particles and move them normally to the propagation direction, demonstrating the independent motion of particles/joints subgroups [Maas and Johansson 1971], despite particles (or their vicinities) sharing their standing wave axes.

Enhancing the representation of levitated characters with volumetric visual content, physical artifacts [Ezcurdia et al. 2022; Gao et al. 2023] or other modalities such as haptics and parametric audio, can provide unique storytelling experiences, where for example volumetric characters interact or talk in mid-air. We also anticipate that our work will enable even more novel multi-particle animations, as real-world visualisations based on acoustic levitation have now reached a wider audience [Li et al. 2022].

2 RELATED WORK AND BACKGROUND

For over a decade, acoustic levitation has been drawning attention within the graphics community due to its ability to generate graphic content in mid-air [Ochiai et al. 2014]. Levitated particles act as voxels or anchors that hold a projection screen [Morales et al. 2019]. In fast-forward, i.e., high-speed particle movement, voxels can draw volumetric shapes [Fushimi et al. 2019; Hirayama et al. 2019] and a rotating levitated screen can provide volumetric projections [Hirayama et al. 2022].

Compared to other novel 3D display technologies, such as optical holographic near-eye displays [Chakravarthula et al. 2019], representing information with acoustic point displays is still limited. Yet they offer some unique advantages. First, they do not require additional equipment. Second, they do not present images with depth cues, but rather display artifacts that occupy space and can interact with physical objects [Hirayama et al. 2022]. Third, they provide multisensory experiences, combining visual content with haptic feedback and audible sound [Hirayama et al. 2019; Plasencia et al. 2020]. Finally, acoustic levitation can manipulate all types of materials as well as complex structures, finding application in contactless assembly and fabrication [Ezcurdia et al. 2022].

2.1 Acoustic holography

An acoustic hologram $\boldsymbol {x}\in {\mathbb {C}^N}$ involves N sources with amplitude and phase activations an ∈ [0, 1], φn ∈ [ − π, π], n = 0, … , N, so that x = a○ejφ (○ denotes element-wise multiplication). It generates a spatial field of complex pressure at M points in space via a linear forward model F(x) = Fx. In point holography, $\boldsymbol {F} \in {\mathbb {C}{}^{M \times N}}$ includes the individual source-to-point complex valued contributions. For a spherical source, $F_{mn}=\frac{e^{jkr_{mn}}}{r_{mn}}$, where rmn is the source-point distance and k is the operating frequency wavenumber. For ultrasonic transducers in phased arrays, there is also a directivity amplitude factor given by the piston model [Plasencia et al. 2020]. Note that the conjugate transpose of F gives the backward propagation of a field $\boldsymbol {p}\in {\mathbb {C}^M}$, i.e., B(p) = F*p.

For acoustic levitation in symmetric setups, e.g., when no arbitrary obstacles are involved [Hirayama et al. 2022], it is common to generate high amplitude focal points at trapping locations similar to haptic applications (M ≪ N and is equal to the number of traps). Standard holographic signatures convert them into acoustic traps [Marzo et al. 2015]. It has been shown that trapping quality linearly correlates with focal amplitude [Plasencia et al. 2020].

While acoustic focal points or traps have established amplitude patterns, their time of arrival, expressed by their phase, is not available. For this reason, acoustic CGH has been solving PR problems

\begin{equation} \underset {\boldsymbol {x}}{\text{min }} \mathcal {L}(|\boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x})|;\boldsymbol {y}) + \mathcal {A}(|\boldsymbol {x}|;\boldsymbol {1}) \end{equation}
(1)
to generate fields that match some target amplitude y. Here $\mathcal {L}$ is an acoustic field amplitude objective. For the most common phase-only holograms, the transducer amplitude constraint $\mathcal {A}$ is an indicator function that is zero if |x| = 1 and infinite otherwise.

For differentiable objectives, the problem in Eq. (1) is addressed by projected (or proximal) gradient descent (PGD) that iterates

\begin{equation} \boldsymbol {x}^{i+1} =\mathcal {P}_{\mathcal {A},\boldsymbol {1}} \left(\boldsymbol {x}^{i} - \tau \nabla _{\boldsymbol {x}} \mathcal {L}(\boldsymbol {x}^{i})\right) \end{equation}
(2)
In Eq. (2), τ is a step size for the gradient step, and the projection $\mathcal {P}_{\mathcal {A},\boldsymbol {\alpha }}(\boldsymbol {x})=\boldsymbol {a}\circ \frac{\boldsymbol {x}}{|\boldsymbol {x}|}$ keeps the phase of the gradient step and imposes a unity amplitude, to satisfy the non-smooth $\mathcal {A}$. The gradient in Eq. (2) is given by
\begin{equation} \nabla _{\boldsymbol {x}} \mathcal {L}(\boldsymbol {x})=\left(\frac{\partial \boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x})}{\partial \boldsymbol {x}}\right)^{*} \left(\frac{\partial |\boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x})|}{\partial \boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x})}\right)^{*} \left(\frac{\partial \mathcal {L}(|\boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x})|)}{\partial |\boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x})|}\right)^{*} \end{equation}
(3)

However, the modulus function in Eq. (1) is not differentiable. Early works in phase retrieval addressed this using indicator amplitude functions for both terms, i.e. $\mathcal {L}(|\boldsymbol {F} (\boldsymbol {x})|)=\mathcal {A}(|\boldsymbol {F} (\boldsymbol {x})|;\boldsymbol {y})$ [Bauschke et al. 2002]. Satisfying these non-smooth terms via alternating amplitude projections leads to the GS algorithm [Gerchberg 1972] or iterative backpropagation (IBP) [Marzo and Drinkwater 2019] for acoustic point holography.

\begin{equation} \boldsymbol {x}^{i+1} = \mathcal {P}_{\mathcal {A},\boldsymbol {1}}\biggl (\boldsymbol {B}\Bigl (\mathcal {P}_{\mathcal {A},\boldsymbol {y}}\bigl (\boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x}^{i})\bigr)\Bigr)\biggr) \end{equation}
(4)
Note that Eq. (4) includes the 4 known GS steps: a) the forward propagation of the hologram F(xi), b) the target field amplitude constraint $\mathcal {P}_{\mathcal {A},\boldsymbol {y}}\Bigl (\boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x}^{i}))\Bigr)=\boldsymbol {y}\circ \frac{\boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x}^{i}))}{|\boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x}^{i})|}$, c) its backward propagation, and, finally, d) the hologram's amplitude constraint.

In point holography, many methods have attempted to estimate focal phases without two propagations (forward and backward) per iteration [Long et al. 2014; Plasencia et al. 2020], which enables extremely fast implementations. The simplest choice, termed NAÏVE, is to assume zero phase for all points and compute the hologram as $\boldsymbol {x_{NAIVE}} = \mathcal {P}_{\mathcal {A},\boldsymbol {1}}\Bigl (\boldsymbol {B}(\boldsymbol {y})\Bigr)$. On the other hand, GS-PAT omitted the target field amplitude constraint in GS (IBP) and iterated over the smallest point domain to estimate the focal phases. However, all these methods including IBP (Eq. (4)) fail to deliver uniform amplitudes according to the target y [Plasencia et al. 2020].

More recently, the Wirtinger calculus allowed computing the gradient $\frac{\partial |\boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x})|}{\partial \boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x})}$ [Candes et al. 2015], enabling PGD for differentiable objectives $\mathcal {L}$ with respect to the amplitude |F(x)|. Gradient descent methods have mostly been using the real part of $\left(\frac{\partial \boldsymbol {x}(\boldsymbol {\varphi })}{\partial \boldsymbol {\varphi }}\right)^{*}\nabla _{\boldsymbol {x}} \mathcal {L}(\boldsymbol {x})$ to directly update the holograms’ phase. While slower than GS, they have been more accurate in delivering pressure [Fushimi et al. 2021] or optimizing Gor'kov potential related quantities [Hirayama et al. 2022; Marzo et al. 2015], which use more control points per trap to determine acoustic trap quality.

2.2 Dynamic acoustic levitation

All these methods do not include any temporal dependence while computing holograms for each time frame of a multi-particle trajectory. As a result, the magnitude of phase change (or signed angular difference defined in [ − π, π]) for any transducer can be very high among successive frames. High phase changes have been identified as reasons for audible noise in PATs [Hoshi 2016] and transducer amplitude fluctuation [Suzuki et al. 2020]. To better realize this issue, consider a PAT operating at 40 kHz and updated every 10 kHz. The transducers’ driving signals for each update last only 4 periods, and temporally shifting them to realize large phase delays, would severely distort them. As a result, transducers are pushed away from their operating frequency and thus emit weaker. We demonstrate this phase change induced frequency shift with measurements in Figure 5 shown in the all figure bottom pages.

Following these, the success of multi-particle trajectories is currently subject to their prior careful selection, so that transducers do not exhibit high phase changes. Intuitively, this phase change effect is more obvious for particles moving along the same standing wave axis, as their standing wave patterns go in (or out of) phase, when their distance is equal (or different) to an integer multiple of the wavelength. Nevertheless, here we will expose this issue with an example where particles do not share standing wave axes.

Throughout this work, we consider a 16 × 16 top-bottom PAT setup (N = 512) shown in Figure 2. We quantize holograms for 128 levels of phase modulation [Hirayama et al. 2019], so we consider the sum of absolute phase changes $\Bigl (sum(|\boldsymbol {\Delta \varphi (x)}|)\Bigr)$ as a measure, and the minimum change π/64 as a unit, for quantifying phase change. Thus, in Figure 2, a value 1 on the y-axis of the plots means that only one transducer changed by ± π/64, while 512 could mean that all transducers changed by ± π/64, or 8 transducers changed by ± π (which would cause different fluctuation), etc.

We compute holograms using the NAÏVE and a PR algorithm (here IBP) for two test trajectories, where 4 particles forming a horizontal line, start moving with a constant velocity of 0.1 m/s normally to transducer propagation. First, all particles move towards the same direction, while pairs of particles move in opposing directions in the second case. For same direction movements, both solvers provide an almost constant amount of phase change over time to realize constant particle displacements (here 0.01 mm for 10kHz updates). When particles move in opposing directions, the PR algorithm shows phase change peaks at multiple time frames.

Consequently, all multi-particle 3D trajectories of the same class as Figure 2 b, are deemed impossible when using time-invariant PR. Gradually shifting phase with smaller increments until full phase switch is achieved [Hoshi 2016; Suzuki et al. 2020] has been proposed for smoothing phase change. However, interpolation significantly increases the number of transducer updates required for particle displays, eventually reducing their playback speed limit. For example, for a phase increment of π/32, one display frame needs 32 transducer updates to realize the full phase change range.

3 TEMPORAL ACOUSTIC POINT HOLOGRAPHY

In this section, we introduce temporal acoustic CGH, where we both optimize focal amplitudes and suppress transducers’ phase change. We first describe our GS-based hardware implementation for multi-particle dynamic levitation. Last, we also discuss more numerically involved solvers for different objectives and applications.

3.1 Temporal Weighted Gerchberg-Saxton (TWGS)

Following the intuition behind GS, which solves the feasibility problem $\mathcal {A}(|\boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x})|;\boldsymbol {y}) + \mathcal {A}(|\boldsymbol {x}|;\boldsymbol {1})$, finding solutions that intersect two sets (or requirements), here we look for holograms that

  1. have unity amplitude (phase-only) and minimum phase change from the previous frame hologram
  2. generate focal points with the target amplitudes and small phase changes much from the previous frame focal points,

and solve the feasibility problem

\begin{equation} \underset {\boldsymbol {x}}{\text{min }} \mathcal {A}(|\boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x})|;\boldsymbol {y}) + \Theta \Bigl (\boldsymbol {\Delta \theta }\bigl (\boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x})\bigr)\Bigr) + \mathcal {A}(|\boldsymbol {x}|;\boldsymbol {1}) + \Phi \Bigl (\boldsymbol {\Delta \varphi }(\boldsymbol {x})\Bigr) \end{equation}
(5)
where Δφ(x) (or Δθ(F(x))) gives the phase change of hologram x (or focal points F(x)) from its previous frame estimate. The functions Φ, Θ express phase change suppression for transducers and points, respectively. In our implementation, Φ, Θ are indicators that are zero for phase changes smaller or equal to user-defined thresholds. We satisfy these constraints using a phase projection
\begin{equation} \boldsymbol {\Delta \varphi }\left(\mathcal {P}_{\Phi }(\boldsymbol {x})\right) = sign\left(\boldsymbol {\Delta \varphi }(\boldsymbol {x})\right) \circ min\left(|\boldsymbol {\Delta \varphi }(\boldsymbol {x})|,\boldsymbol {\eta }\right) \end{equation}
(6)
that rotates transducers with phase changes |Δφ(xn)| > ηn to the closest acceptable magnitude, i.e. the threshold ηn, or leaves the input intact otherwise. For points’ phase change, we denote this projection as $\boldsymbol {\Delta \theta }\Bigl (\mathcal {P}_{\Theta }\bigl (\boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x})\bigr)\Bigr)$. Figure 7 (bottom pages) shows a flowchart of the modified GS iterations for Eq. (5)
\begin{equation} \boldsymbol {p}^{i}=\mathcal {P}_{\Theta }\mathcal {P}_{\mathcal {A},\boldsymbol {y}}\bigl (\boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x}^{i})\bigr) \end{equation}
(7a)
\begin{equation} \boldsymbol {x}^{i+1}=\mathcal {P}_{\Phi }\mathcal {P}_{\mathcal {A},\boldsymbol {1}}\bigl (\boldsymbol {B}(\boldsymbol {p}^{i})\bigr) \end{equation}
(7b)

and graphically illustrates the amplitude and phase projections. Note that hologram (or field) constraints for amplitude and phase are independent forming a single set, and their projections can be performed in any order, e.g. $\mathcal {P}_{\Phi }\mathcal {P}_{\mathcal {A},\boldsymbol {1}}\bigl (\boldsymbol {B}(\boldsymbol {p}^{i})\bigr)=\mathcal {P}_{\mathcal {A},\boldsymbol {1}}\mathcal {P}_{\Phi }\bigl (\boldsymbol {B}(\boldsymbol {p}^{i})\bigr)$.

3.1.1 Focal amplitude optimization. Compared to SLMs and the popular optical CGH within the graphics community, acoustic holograms have fewer and less directive elements that attenuate after a relatively small distance [Prat-Camps et al. 2020]. Beyond PAT and point holography, diffraction limited holograms have demonstrated more involved aliasing free acoustic fields for manipulation or fabrication of compact structures [Melde et al. 2023; 2016]. Overall though, acoustic holograms cannot yet realize complex scenes, and distributing available acoustic energy to sparse point locations or simple compact shapes has been their main application. From this point of view, acoustic CGH is mostly binary, and the target field y includes weights, with 0 and 1 indicating the "coldest" and "hottest" acoustic regions, respectively.

With this goal in mind, we consider minimizing the objective

\begin{equation} \mathcal {L}_{sum}(x)=-sum\left(\boldsymbol {y}\circ |\boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x})|\right)+\frac{1}{2}sum(|\boldsymbol {x}|^2) \end{equation}
(8)
The first term in Eq. (8) maximizes the sum of amplitudes at the non-zero parts of the target amplitude field. For static holography (Eq. (1)), we can compute $\left(\frac{\partial \mathcal {L}_{sum}(|\boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x})|)}{\partial |\boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x})|)}\right)^{*}=-\boldsymbol {y}$, and according to the Wirtinger calculus, $\frac{\partial |\boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x})|}{\partial \boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x})}=diag\Bigl (\frac{\boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x})}{|\boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x})|}\Bigr)$ (see Supplementary Material Section 1). Plugging the gradient
\begin{equation} \nabla _{\boldsymbol {x}} \mathcal {L}_{sum}(\boldsymbol {x})=-\boldsymbol {B}\Bigl (\mathcal {P}_{\mathcal {A},\boldsymbol {y}}\bigl (\boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x})\bigr)\Bigr)+\boldsymbol {x} \end{equation}
(9)
in PGD (Eq. (2)) for τ = 1, we recover the iterations of GS (Eq. (4)). Consequently, $\mathcal {L}_{sum}(|\boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x})|)$ and $\mathcal {A}(|\boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x})|;\boldsymbol {y})$ are equivalent when it comes to proximal gradient descent, and GS (Eq. (4)) delivers maximum amplitude at (non-zero) target locations.

The Tikhonov term in $\mathcal {L}_{sum}$ does not affect the acoustic field, but rather promotes complex holograms of minimum energy. This comes in agreement with [Di Leonardo et al. 2007], where for optical tweezers and an invertible (FB = I) Fourier forward model, the authors recognised the GS iteration as a stationary point for the sum of focal amplitudes, given the phase estimate of this iteration. In practice, the Tikhonov term leads the complex hologram estimates to more uniformly distributed amplitudes that remain (almost) unaffected after the amplitude projection $\mathcal {P}_{\mathcal {A},\boldsymbol {1}}$ in Eq. 2. For more involved binary targets like image holography shown in Figure 6 (bottom pages), this significantly accelerates delivering maximum pressure at the non-zero parts of the target (see Supplementary Material Figure 2), but does not affect the smaller point holography problems.

Our findings enable unifying all current acoustic CGH PR algorithms in a single platform, no matter whether this is for software like automatic differentiation [Fushimi et al. 2021] or hardware like OpenMPD [Montano-Murillo et al. 2023]. For example, we can implement the GS algorithm with automatically computed gradients instead of $\nabla _{\boldsymbol {x}} \mathcal {L}_{sum}(\boldsymbol {x})$ (Eq. 9), and temporarily turn off gradients tracking for the non-linear projection to a unity amplitude (see Supplementary Figure 1). The actual difference between CGH algorithms lays on the task they execute, or simply the objective they satisfy, which depends on the application.

As it has been a frequent comparison [Fushimi et al. 2021; Peng et al. 2020], in Figure 6 (bottom pages), we compare WGS with amplitude least squares $\mathcal {L}_{als}(\boldsymbol {x})=\frac{1}{2}sum\Bigl (\bigl (s \boldsymbol {y}-|\boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x})|\bigr)^2\Bigr)$. Here s scales the target field to an exact amplitude estimate that we extract from GS. We use the Weighted GS (WGS) [Di Leonardo et al. 2007], as standard GS delivers maximum amplitude but fails to equalize according to y. WGS achieves both higher amplitude values and uniformity at the non-zero target parts (Figure 6 a), while $\mathcal {L}_{als}$ optimizes both low and high pressure values, achieving better contrasts at the expense of lower and less uniform pressure (Figure 6 b).

On the other hand, in rigid particle levitation, trap locations are well separated (Figure 6 c), while maximum focal amplitudes entail higher trapping stiffness [Plasencia et al. 2020]. Previously used for optical trapping and acoustic compact holography [Xie et al. 2016], WGS can perfectly execute this task. While Figure 6 d refers to the focal point arrangement of Figure 6 c, it is a general trend that WGS generates faster maximum and equalized focal amplitudes (see higher mean amplitude $\Bigl (mean(|\boldsymbol {F(x)})|)\Bigr)$ in Figure 6 d and note a STD of ∼ 10 Pa), setting a limit for the performance of PATs. Amplitude least squares are recommended for exact and more conservative target amplitudes, which are useful in haptic applications or can prevent excessive pressure delivery that causes atomization of levitated droplets [Foresti et al. 2013].

3.1.2 Transducers’ phase change. Based on PAT discrete phase modulation [Hirayama et al. 2019], we would formally seek to minimize $\Phi \Bigl (\boldsymbol {\Delta \varphi }(\boldsymbol {x})\Bigr)=sum\Bigl (|Q\bigl (\boldsymbol {\Delta \varphi }(x)\bigr)|\Bigr)$, where Q maps phase change to L ≤ 129 discrete levels $0,\pm \frac{\pi }{64}, \pm \frac{L-1}{2}\frac{\pi }{64}$. Different than full phase range quantization [Choi et al. 2022], here it is essential to also minimize the sum of absolute phase changes [Nagahara 2015]. This suggests that ideally most (but not all) transducers will not change, and most changes will concentrate at the lower quantization levels.

However, the minimum non-zero phase change for moving particles is not known in advance for arbitrary trajectories. The NAÏVE algorithm can provide an estimate, as it does not introduce additional phase changes for focal amplitude optimization. Besides, for feasible trajectories similar to Figure 2 a using up to 16 particles and up to 1 m/s velocity, we found that $99\%$ of phase changes for all frames are contained only within the first L ≤ 5 discrete levels.

Following these, to provide the necessary phase change amount (or at least a comparable one with the NAÏVE algorithm), and mitigate the transducer amplitude fluctuation caused by high phase changes, we used just 5 discrete levels of modulation, i.e. |Δφ(x)| ≤ π/32. We chose to set all larger phase changes to ± π/32 using Eq. (1), and only quantized to the lowest 5 discrete levels after the iterative part. For our levitation experiments, we found that soft thresholding or quantizing along iterations, show negligible numerical and experimental differences compared to clipping absolute changes larger than π/32 (see Supplementary Material Figures 7, 8).

3.1.3 Focal points’ phase change. Here we assume that as acoustic traps continuously move in space, their phases between successive frames should be similar and relate to their displacements. We consider a single transducer and moving focal point in space. According to the piston model [Plasencia et al. 2020], a single transducer generates phases kr1, kr2 at two successive focal positions, where r1, r2 are the transducer-focal points distances. The phase difference between these points is |Δθ| = |k(r1 − r2)| ≤ kΔ r, where Δ r is the displacement between them, with |Δθ| = kΔ r when a single line connects the transducer and both positions.

We extend this concept to multiple transducers and focal points and set Θ to be an indicator for $\Big |\boldsymbol {\Delta \theta }\bigl (\boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x})\bigr)\Bigr | \le k\boldsymbol {\Delta r}$ where Δr includes particles’ displacements. In Figure 3, for π/32 transducer threshold and 5 algorithm iterations, we examine this constraint's effect on a multi-particle trajectory. Initially forming a 3cm radius circle, 8 particles move to the vertices of a 4cm cube and back to the initial position (see also second example in Figure 8 at the bottom pages). Here, each particle has its own individual constant velocity (maximum 0.05 m/s). In our Supplementary Material, we also provide the simulations of Section 3 for a human motion animation.

We observe that acoustic field amplitude and phase change objectives are antagonistic. For no focal phase constraints (threshold π), we would achieve the highest equalized focal amplitudes if it were not for transducer amplitude fluctuation, e.g. in static holography. Displacement based point thresholds achieve a good trade-off between amplitude optimization and phase change suppression, while more strict phase constraints further reduce focal amplitudes. In our experiments, we primarily focused on eliminating phase change to safeguard mid-air particle trajectories. As a result, we maintained the first frame's focal phases, i.e., set $\boldsymbol {\Delta \theta }\bigl (\boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x})\bigr)=\boldsymbol {0}$, i.e., which performs well for comparable displacements among particles.

3.1.4 Algorithm performance. Our temporal WGS (TWGS) compromises maximum and equalized focal amplitudes of time-invariant WGS with minimum phase change equivalent to the NAÏVE algorithm (Figure 4). Starting each frame's computation from the previous acoustic field and using 5 iterations in a serial implementation, we reached the 10 kHz benchmark for multimodal displays with a standard 16 × 16 top-bottom PAT setup [Plasencia et al. 2020], enabling particle displays of potentially equal frame rate. Our algorithm provides holograms with smaller phase change range, and thus also favors further use of phase interpolation, reducing its required transducer updates per display frame. Facilitating temporal dependence with a serial implementation is a limitation of the presented approach. Our future plans involve batch implementations, which though involve the subtlety that previous holograms used for computing phase change, will also be changing across iterations.

3.2 Other applications and solvers

Our algorithmic choices so far reflected the current hardware reality for multimodal particle displays. According to this, amplitude and position modulation haptics as well as volumetric visual content require ≥ 3kHz position updates [Montano-Murillo et al. 2023], which makes computational time a major decision factor. Consequently, for our levitation experiments, we chose TWGS with early stopping to rapidly generate maximum and equalized focal amplitudes. Next, we used hard phase clipping constraints to shrink the feasible solution space. Especially the point constraint Θ breaks the differentiable chain for PGD (Eq. 3), to control the trade-off between amplitude and phase objectives in very few iterations.

Beyond these extreme hardware requirements, here we optimize $\mathcal {L}_{als}(\boldsymbol {x})$ to generate exact and slightly conservative focal amplitudes. With application in droplets’ levitation or haptics delivery, this is a more interesting numerical problem, as gradient descent requires many iterations to achieve this goal (Figure 6 d). We suppress the sum of (absolute) changes $\Phi \bigl (\boldsymbol {\Delta \varphi (\boldsymbol {x})}\bigr)=sum(|\boldsymbol {\Delta \varphi (x)}|)$, which is effectively a sparsity constraint [Nagahara 2015]. Least squares with sparsity constraints have been standard optimization routines that have been the subject of domain-specific languages [Heide et al. 2016]. Beyond this, here we remove the small numerical effect of quantization to focus on the optimization of $\mathcal {L}_{als}(\boldsymbol {x}) + \Phi \bigl (\boldsymbol {\Delta \varphi (\boldsymbol {x})}\bigr)$, where $\mathcal {L}_{als}, \Phi$ are conflicting.

In Figure 3 we showed how intensive phase change suppression compromises amplitude optimization. Reducing the acoustic field objective $\mathcal {L}$ often comes through increased (absolute) phased changes. On the other hand, for minimum (zero or too small) phase changes, the acoustic field does not change enough to realize dynamic movements for focal points. To more formally address the optimization of antagonistic objectives [Marchesini 2007], we depart from gradient descent and introduce saddle point phase retrieval in CGH. Instead of treating $\mathcal {L}\bigl (|\boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x})|\bigr)+\Phi \bigl (\Delta \varphi (\boldsymbol {x})\bigr)$ as a whole, we consider $\mathcal {L},\Phi$ as conflicting maximization and minimization objectives. We find saddle points using the Primal Dual Hybrid Gradient (PDHG) algorithm [Chambolle and Pock 2011; Goldstein et al. 2023]. In our Supplementary Material Section 3.2, we derive PDHG for $\mathcal {L}_{als}(|\boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x})|)$ and $\mathcal {A}(|\boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x})|;s\boldsymbol {y})$, which interestingly show an equivalence when it comes to PDHG.

We evaluate PDHG and gradient based methods for this problem in two initialization conditions; a) starting from the previous time frame phases and b) from zero phases for all transducers at all frames. We compare PDHG with a modification of the iterative soft thresholding algorithm (ISTA) [Beck and Teboulle 2009], which is the standard proximal gradient approach for non-smooth constraints, as well as direct gradient descent for $\mathcal {L}\bigl (|\boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x})|\bigr)+\Phi \bigl (\Delta \varphi (\boldsymbol {x})\bigr)$ via automatic differentiation (AD).

For the spatially continuous dynamic levitation and the trajectory examined in Figure 3, the previous hologram provides a good starting point, as Δφ(x) = 0, and the focal amplitude maximum is spatially close to the previous. As shown in Figure 9 a and Figure 10 a (bottom pages), all methods converge to focal amplitudes of 5000 Pa and phase changes often lower than the NAÏVE algorithm. In our experiments, both PDHG and ISTA successfully reproduced this multiparticle trajectory without significant differences.

In practice, this is an appropriate initialization, as previous phases are always present in computing phase change along iterations. However, we also test algorithms in more demanding conditions, i.e., starting from zero phases in each frame. PDHG generates moving focal points following optimization paths different than descent direction that can fall into local minima. Indeed, as shown in Figure 9 b and Figure 10 b, only PDHG can address both objectives from a worse starting point, showing though slightly larger phase changes than NAÏVE and a handful of peaks in a movement of 20000 frames. Safeguarding phase change in lower acceptable levels (here $|\boldsymbol {\Delta \varphi (\boldsymbol {x})}| \le \pi$), and more careful regularization for frames with phase change anomalies could eliminate these peaks.

In Supplementary Material Section 3.2, we describe implementations and parameter tuning for all methods of this subsection. We used 500 iterations for both gradient based methods, and up to 5000 iterations for PDHG, unless termination conditions from [Goldstein et al. 2023] are met. Although slower, PDHG was the only method to deal with both objectives regardless of the starting point. We anticipate that saddle-point optimization will be more critical for discontinuous dynamic acoustic fields (e.g. when we suddenly deliver acoustic energy to previously “cold” acoustic regions), as well as other CGH applications where antagonistic objectives arise.

4 MULTI-PARTICLE 3D ANIMATIONS

4.1 Acoustic levitation system

Our levitation system is based on OpenMPD [Montano-Murillo et al. 2023], an open hardware and software framework for multi-modal acoustic displays, where instructions on assemblying an acoustophoretic setup and embedding the required firmware are also provided1. Besides, experimental setups similar to the one used in this work are currently commercially available2.

The PATs used are composed of 16 × 16 Murata MA40S4S transducers (40 kHz, 10 mm in diameter, delivering ∼ 6 Pa at 1 m distance when driven at 20 Vpp). For levitation experiments and simulations, we used the standard top-bottom setup [Marzo and Drinkwater 2019; Plasencia et al. 2020], where two aligned PATs (in the xy plane) face each other, here with a separation of 24 cm in the z-axis. We levitated expanded polystyrene beads (2-3 mm diameter).

The OpenMPD software provides several levitation algorithms including NAÏVE, IBP, and GS-PAT. Thanks to its OpenCL-based GPU implementation, the software allows multi-point levitation at different update rates up to 10 kHz with all these solvers. We implemented our temporal PR solver in OpenMPD to allow comparisons. Unlike other algorithms, our TWGS does not support parallel computation of multiple frames, because it requires phase information from previous frames to constrain phase changes. Nevertheless, we achieved 10 kHz updates with 10 points owing to the small number of iterations used. Note that the IBP implementation in OpenMPD currently supports the creation of up to 8 traps, and thus we did not use IBP for experiments with 10 or 16 particles.

4.2 Shape animations

We first demonstrate our algorithm's capabilities in various shape animations, for which we anticipated behaviour like Figure 2 b for time-invariant PR. Figure 8 (bottom pages) shows the 6 designed trajectories, which involve 4, 8, or 16 particles, vertically aligning or following opposing directions along the transducer propagation. Among them are the examples in Figure 2 b and Figure 4. We used the same path planning algorithm (i.e., S2M2 [Chen et al. 2021]) as in [Gao et al. 2023] to avoid particle collisions. Particles’ positions are updated at 10 kHz, and each particle moves in linear segments with its own constant velocity (up to 0.1 m/s).

Time-invariant PR algorithms like GS-PAT and IBP failed all our experiments. On the other hand, we observed that the NAÏVE algorithm successfully carried out the 4-particle trajectories, but expectedly showed particle drops or drags to secondary traps as particles increased. Achieving high equalized focal amplitudes and low phase changes of the same order as the NAÏVE algorithm (Figure 4), TWGS succeeded all test trajectories.

4.3 Human character animations

Additionally, we demonstrate character animations, where levitated particles indicate human body joints. In our experiments, we considered 10 of the 19 particles comprising a human body in the examples of MoVi dataset [Ghorbani et al. 2021]. We believe human motion is a good benchmark assessment for unrestricted 3D particle displays, as it decomposes to a main motion among all particles plus independent motions of joints/particles subgroups [Maas and Johansson 1971]. We placed characters so that they move normally to the transducer propagation direction, which means that particles need to move independently while almost aligning in the z-axis with arbitrary separations, emulating test cases like Figure 2 b.

We extracted 5 motion examples from [Ghorbani et al. 2021], which include clips of walking, lateral walking, (in-and-out) jumping, running, and kicking by a point display forming a human person. Translating the dataset to acoustic levitation coordinate input included individually translating joints, so that all particles have a minimum separation of 2 wavelengths at all times. Thus, characters were not identical in all examples. Last, we used spline interpolation between frames successive particles’ positions, so that their maximum displacement is less than 0.1 mm at all times. For a conservative 500 Hz particle position update, which corresponds to a maximum velocity 0.05 m/s, all human motions were successful.

In our experiments, we initially place 10 particles on a xy-plane circle at an equal distance from both arrays. We transfer particles to the initial human body arrangement via paths planned by S2M2. We should note that for human motion, position/movement updates are not common with transducer updates as in S2M2 paths. The transducers receive an update every 10 kHz, but phases change at movement frequency. We annotate particles’ positions for each animation frame with corresponding timings that define motion speed and feed this data to the OpenMPD engine. For each algorithm (GS-PAT, NAÏVE, TWGS), we repeat the experiments 5 times at a relatively low speed (2 ms between animation frames, i.e., 500 Hz movement update rate) and report significantly fewer particle drops for our algorithm in Table 1. Besides, apart from the jumping animation that occupied more volume and traps are slightly weaker, we achieved 2 kHz or more in all motions.

It is worth noting that for holograms computed at 10 kHz and an increment of π/32, phase interpolation for the full phase change range would be limited to 312.5 Hz. However, phase interpolation can be valuable for tuning the amplitude/phase change trade-off. In this work, we set phase change suppression to NAÏVE algorithm levels as our first priority to prevent transducer amplitude fluctuation, often at the expense of focal amplitude optimization (e.g. Figure 3). Relaxing phase change constraints and using a minimum number of interpolated frames can allow a solution that better optimizes focal amplitudes.

5 DISCUSSION

In this work, we considered dynamic CGH problems of the form $\mathcal {L}\bigl (|\boldsymbol {F}(\boldsymbol {x})|\bigr)+\Phi \bigl (\boldsymbol {\Delta \varphi }(\boldsymbol {x})\bigr)$, where $\mathcal {L}$ is an acoustic field amplitude objective and Φ basically enforces sparsity on the phase change between successive holograms. We numerically and experimentally demonstrated how phase change suppression in PATs, enabled presenting unique graphic content with rigid levitated particles. Formulating acoustic CGH as a sparsity problem for phase change can also ease the reconfiguration of diffraction limited spatial sound modulators [Hardwick et al. 2023], and lead advances in dynamic compact CGH.

We interpreted the widely used GS algorithm as projected gradient descent for maximizing focal amplitudes, and demonstrated it is the fastest and most efficient way to distribute all acoustic energy to a sparse set of points for dynamic levitation. For the phase change sparsity constraint, this corresponds to the most standard L1 proximal gradient descent algorithm [Heide et al. 2016]. In applications where the delivered dynamic acoustic field is not continuous, like audio and haptics delivery [Montano-Murillo et al. 2023], satisfying both $\mathcal {L}$ and Φ can become more demanding. Departing from gradient descent methods, we introduced the PDHG algorithm and saddle-point optimization for PR in CGH, which can be valuable for optimizing such antagonistic objectives [Marchesini 2007].

However, modern algorithms for non-smooth optimization, like PDHG or the Alternative Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM) are very slow, especially for the update rates of multimodal particle displays. For example, most ADMM formulations, including optical CGH [Choi 2021], involve an iterative method within each main iteration, which is prohibitive for point holograms. On the contrary, alternating projection algorithms, including ADMM equivalents [Elser et al. 2018], are still state-of-the-art in applications like X-ray crystallography, where one seeks signals of known support (i.e., non-zero regions). With acoustic CGH being itself about synthesizing a field of known support, future research should focus on developing or learning [Diamond et al. 2017] computationally efficient projection schemes that will better express the singularities of acoustic holographic fields.

A more general challenge for short-time dynamic point holography is to compute accurate forward models for simulation and iterative optimization. For example, it is currently difficult to distinct the performance between red and black lines in Figure 3, as the phase change impact on actual pressure is not yet properly quantified. Estimating the fundamental frequencies for each transducer and time frame (see Figure 5), and then superposing multi-frequency fields is a potential solution, which should though be cross-validated with acoustic field measurements. However, during iterative computations, this would not just require recomputing the forward model in each iteration, but also frequency estimation on the fly.

Nevertheless, our "monochromatic" implementation enabled previously impossible graphic content using levitated particles. Due to the independent movement of particle subgroups in 3D space and the available datasets [Ghorbani et al. 2021], human motion can provide benchmark comparisons for advancing dynamic multi-particle levitation, as well as a valuable format of visual representation. More precise control of particles will reveal useful information about levitated characters, such as their physical attributes, emotions, or intentions [Troje 2002], while levitated sticks [Ezcurdia et al. 2022] or other artifacts/materials can enhance their visual display. Combined with other modalities such as parametric audio, levitated characters could also talk or interact in different ways beyond movement. This opens the door to new experiences in storytelling for entertainment, similar to the muppet theatre or shadow plays, but of course without any strings attached.
